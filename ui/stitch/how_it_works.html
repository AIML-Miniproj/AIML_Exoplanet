The problem

Most dips are
not planets

When a planet passes in front of its star, the star looks a tiny bit dimmer for a few hours. NASA's TESS telescope records the brightness of hundreds of thousands of stars, so it sees these repeating dips all the time.

The catch: a dip is not proof of a planet. Two stars eclipsing each other, a star that pulses or has dark spots, and instrument noise can all make dips that look the same at first glance. Checking every one by eye is far too slow.

In short
This project sorts every dip into one of four answers (planet, eclipsing binary, variable star, noise) and explains why.

Step 1 · Collect

Gather the
starlight

You pick a star. Its name is turned into a TESS catalog number (a TIC ID), and its light curves are downloaded from NASA's MAST archive: a brightness reading every 2 minutes, already cleaned of known spacecraft problems by NASA's own pipeline.

TESS watches each patch of sky for about 27 days, a sector. Many stars are seen in several sectors, so the pipeline downloads them all (up to 10 in the app) and saves them on disk, so the same data is never downloaded twice.

To learn what each kind of signal looks like, the project also uses public catalogs: NASA's TOI list for planets, the TESS eclipsing-binary catalog, Gaia's list of variable stars, and the TESS Input Catalog for each star's size and temperature.

02
Step 2 · Clean

Remove the wobble,
keep the dips

Raw light curves are messy. Each sector is divided by its own average brightness so they line up. Sudden upward spikes, such as cosmic-ray hits, are cut out, but only upward ones: an early version also cut downward points and accidentally deleted about two thirds of a real planet's dips.

Then a smoothing filter called biweight (from the Wotan library) follows the slow wobble of the star and the spacecraft and removes it. Its window is long (at least about 0.75 days), so short dips pass through untouched; if a strong dip is found, the filter is run again with the dip masked so its depth is not reduced.

lightbulb
In short
Two versions are kept: one flattened for finding dips, and one that still shows the star's own variability.
03
Step 3 · Search

Find the
repeating dip

The search method is Box Least Squares (BLS). Imagine sliding a box-shaped dip along the light curve, repeating it every P days, and checking how well it fits. This is tried for thousands of periods between 0.5 and 30 days and twelve dip lengths.

The period with the best fit wins, but only if it stands out clearly from the rest: its score, the SDE, must be at least 7. Stars that pulse quickly get a second, cleaned copy searched too, used only when it gives a clearly stronger result.

lightbulb
In short
Result: the best period, how deep and how long the dip is, and when it happens.
04
Step 4 · Measure

Describe the signal
with 56 features

A dip on its own says little, so the pipeline measures it from every angle and writes down 56 features:

  • straightenShape: U-shaped or V-shaped, how long compared with what is expected for this star, how big the object would be.
  • compare_arrowsTwo-star tests: do odd and even dips match? Is there a second dip halfway through the orbit?
  • view_weekConsistency: is the dip the same depth in every sector?
  • wavesThe star itself: does it rise and fall in smooth cycles (Lomb-Scargle, autocorrelation, Fourier)?
  • starStar facts: temperature, size, gravity and brightness from the TESS Input Catalog.

In short
Features that grow with more data (like raw signal strength) are converted to per-dip versions, so 2 sectors and 10 sectors give consistent answers.

05
Step 5 · Decide

The AI makes
its call

The 56 features go into XGBoost, a machine-learning model made of 400 small decision trees that vote together. It learned from 400 stars whose nature is already known from the catalogs: 100 planets, 100 eclipsing binaries, 100 variable stars and 100 noise stars, chosen with similar brightness so the model cannot cheat by brightness alone.

Its raw votes are often over-confident, so they are calibrated (Platt scaling): when it says “70% planet”, roughly 7 out of 10 such cases really are planets.

Why not a neural network? With a few hundred examples, small trees work better, handle missing values naturally, and can explain every single decision.

lightbulb
In short
Output: a probability for each of the four answers.
06
Step 6 · Double-check

Physics gets
the final word

Some facts are certain and should not be left to a model trained on 400 stars. A router checks the answer against physics:

  • • If the dip means the object is bigger than 2.5 Jupiters, it cannot be a planet.
  • • If there is a clear second eclipse halfway through the orbit, it is two stars.
  • • If no answer reaches 50%, the star is kept as Needs review instead of being thrown away.

Real example: for TOI 184.01, the first model (100 stars) leaned towards “planet” (66%), and a clear second eclipse at phase 0.50 made the router call it an eclipsing binary, which matches NASA's catalog. The current model gets it right on its own (binary, 88%) and lists the second eclipse as its first reason.

lightbulb
In short
The model suggests; physics can overrule; doubtful cases are kept, not discarded.

Step 8 · Explain

Every verdict
says why

A verdict you cannot question is not much use. For every star, the app writes a short “Why?” list in plain sentences.

The physics checks come first, because they can overrule the model. Then come the measurements that pushed the model most, found with SHAP, a method that measures exactly how much each number moved the answer. Finally, the verdict and the period are compared with NASA's catalog, and any disagreement is shown, not hidden.

In short
The top drivers make physical sense: how regularly the light repeats, the dip's shape and whether its length fits the star.

Step 9 · Results

Results

Every star was scored by a model that had never seen it (5-fold cross-validation, grouped by star), and every model below used the same 400 stars (100 per class) and the same folds. Scores can still vary by about ±0.05 on new stars. The headline: going from 100 to 400 training stars lifted the main score from 0.76 to 0.87; two extra classifiers, several ensembles and feature selection were also tested, and none beat calibrated XGBoost, which stays the model the app uses.

Honest limits

Where not to trust it

dataset
Small dataset
400 stars (100 per class) make the scores much steadier than the first 100, but this is still an academic-scale set. Scores could shift by about ±0.05 on new stars.
light_mode
Brightness only
It cannot rule out a fainter eclipsing binary sitting next to the star on the sky; that needs pixel-level or telescope follow-up.
contrast
Equal eclipses
When both eclipses of a binary look the same, it looks like one dip at half the true period (TOI 1385.01).
brightness_6
Starspots
Spots grow and fade within weeks, so a spotted star's period can come out at a multiple of the true one.
graphic_eq
Noise vs variable
These two overlap most; noise has the lowest score (F1 0.81).
tune
Training range
Trained on 2-minute data, TESS magnitude 8–12 and periods up to 15 days; outside that, be careful.
