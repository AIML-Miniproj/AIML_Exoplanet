Planet or impostor?

Two real demo stars
Planet: HD 209458 b
Planet

A gas giant passes in front of its star every 3.5 days. The dip is short, round-bottomed and exactly the same every time.

Phase: -0.05 T0 (Mid-Transit) Phase: +0.05
Orbital period 3.525 days
Limb Darkening 1.38 %
Size 1.35 Jupiters
  • check_circle Same depth every time: odd and even dips match, so the period is right.
  • check_circle Nothing halfway between: no second dip at phase 0.5, because a planet is far too dark to show one.
TIC 420814525 Classifier: 98% planet
Impostor: TOI 184.01
Eclipsing binary

TESS flagged it as a possible planet, but it is two stars taking turns to block each other's light.

Phase: 0.0 (Primary) Phase: 0.25 Phase: 0.5 (Secondary)
Orbital period 4.817 days
Main eclipse 2.24 % deep
Second eclipse at phase 0.50
  • warning Two dips per orbit: a deep one when the cooler star hides the hotter one, and a faint one half an orbit later.
  • warning Physics agrees: the classifier calls it a binary (88%), and a clear second eclipse half an orbit later confirms it. (The first, 100-star model leaned towards planet, and this physics rule corrected it.)
TIC 300871545 Router: eclipsing binary
psychology

Physics rule: a second eclipse with SNR of 7 or more, or an implied size above 2.5 Jupiter radii, overrides the classifier. Signals with no class above 50% are kept as Needs review instead of being thrown away.

Glossary

14 key ideas

Showing 14 ideas used in this project
Stars & Planets outside our Sun

Exoplanet

A planet that orbits a star other than our Sun. More than 5,500 have been confirmed so far.

In this project

This is the Planet class. The Planet module estimates its size, orbit and temperature, each with an error bar.

The Data since 2018

TESS

NASA's planet-hunting space telescope. Its four cameras watch one large patch of sky for about 27 days, then move on to the next.

In this project

All our data comes from TESS, downloaded from NASA's MAST archive with the lightkurve library.

The Data ~27 days

Sector

One TESS observing window of about 27 days. Some stars are watched in many sectors, others in just one or two.

In this project

We stitch several sectors together: more sectors means more dips to check. The Analyzer shows how many each star used.

Light & Dips brightness vs time

Light curve

A graph of a star's brightness over time. TESS measures it every 2 minutes for the stars we use.

In this project

The Light Curves tab shows each star before and after cleaning, with the sectors shaded.

Light & Dips the dip

Transit

The small, repeating dip in brightness when a planet passes in front of its star.

In this project

Everything starts with a search for these dips; most of them turn out not to be planets.

Light & Dips δ ≈ (Rp / R★)²

Transit depth

How much light is blocked. A Jupiter-sized planet blocks about 1% of a Sun-like star's light; an Earth-sized one about 0.01%.

In this project

From the depth and the star's size we get the object's size. Bigger than 2.5 Jupiters means it cannot be a planet.

Light & Dips P, in days

Orbital period

The time between two dips: how long one trip around the star takes.

In this project

We search periods from 0.5 to 30 days. For binaries, the Binary module decides between the found period and twice it.

Light & Dips flatten

Cleaning (detrending)

Removing slow brightness drifts, from the star or the spacecraft, so the short dips stand out.

In this project

We clip outliers and flatten each sector with a 'biweight' filter (wotan) that leaves the dips untouched.

The AI SDE ≥ 7

BLS search

Box Least Squares: slide a box-shaped dip along the light curve at thousands of trial periods and see which one fits best.

In this project

Our main dip finder (BLS Detection tab). The best period must stand out with a detection score (SDE) of at least 7.

The AI stack the orbits

Phase folding

Cutting the light curve at every orbit and stacking the pieces, so all the dips line up on top of each other.

In this project

The Phase-Folded tab: a planet gives one U-shaped dip; a binary often shows a second dip at phase 0.5.

Stars & Planets two stars

Eclipsing binary

Two stars orbiting each other, lined up so that they take turns blocking each other's light.

In this project

The most common impostor. The Binary module finds its true period, both eclipses and whether the stars are apart or touching.

Stars & Planets smooth cycles

Variable star

A star whose brightness changes on its own: it pulsates, like RR Lyrae stars, or has dark spots that rotate into view.

In this project

Found with a Lomb-Scargle periodogram, which looks for smooth, regular cycles instead of short dips.

The AI 56 features

XGBoost classifier

A machine-learning model made of many small decision trees that vote together.

In this project

It reads 56 features of the signal and gives a calibrated probability for planet, binary, variable and noise.

The AI explainable

SHAP (the “Why?”)

A method that shows how much each measurement pushed the model towards its answer.

In this project

The “Why?” list on the Results tab: physics checks first, then the measurements that mattered most, in plain sentences.
